Recurring inferential problems

How should uncertainty be propagated when the response law or likelihood is non-standard?

Generalized linear, hierarchical, and likelihood-free models for binary outcomes, positive responses, overdispersed counts, simulators, and experiment-level systematics.

What can be inferred from incomplete, selective, or heterogeneous calibration data?

Support mismatch, selective sampling, changing measurement errors, spectroscopic follow-up, and partial identification.

Which representations retain the variation required by the scientific question?

Principal components, graphs, non-negative matrix factorisation, transfer learning, and tests of representation completion.

How can geometry and ordered structure be recovered from resolved observations?

Ridges on curved domains, spectro-spatial segmentation, centre-conditioned radial paths, and ordered spectral-line paths.

Which computations and observations are worth acquiring at survey scale?

QR-based dimension reduction, active-learning follow-up, catalogue construction, and scalable analysis of maps, images, spectra, and data cubes.

Response distributions and likelihood-free inference

Astronomical responses do not share a common sampling law. Star formation may be represented by a binary outcome, a photometric redshift by a positive continuous response, a cluster population by overdispersed counts, and a cosmological calculation by a simulator whose likelihood cannot be evaluated.

The generalized-linear-model series linked each response to an explicit distribution. COSMOABC used population Monte Carlo approximate Bayesian computation when cluster-count simulations were available but the likelihood was not. Hierarchical analyses of thermonuclear rates modelled experiment-specific systematics jointly with the reaction rate.

Selected evidence: GLM series (2015) · COSMOABC (2015) · Bayesian Models for Astrophysical Data (2017) · thermonuclear reaction-rate analyses (2019–2022)

Calibration transfer and spectroscopic selection

A model can perform well on held-out spectra while failing on the photometric population to which its estimates will be applied. The Teddy and Happy catalogues separated failures caused by missing colour–magnitude support from those caused by survey-like photometric errors and selection.

RESSPECT treated transient follow-up as a sequential decision over the candidate pool, class information, observing cost, and remaining telescope budget. Current work asks which population quantities remain identifiable when additional spectroscopy cannot repair missing support.

Selected evidence: Teddy and Happy (2017) · active learning and RESSPECT (2019–2020) · incomplete calibration samples (current)

Spectra as ordered and low-rank objects

Directions that explain the largest variance need not separate transient classes, retain a weak spectral component, or encode the physical parameter of interest. Early work used robust and kernel principal components, transfer learning, and DRACULA for sparse, heterogeneous supernova spectra.

A graph later ordered 1,595 spectra from 145 Type II supernovae by similarity. qrpca accelerates principal-component calculations, SpectralUnmix estimates non-negative components with optional smoothness regularisation, and spectropath encodes line morphology as an ordered path in velocity–flux space.

Selected evidence: DRACULA (2015–2016) · qrpca (2022) · graph-based Type II spectral ordering (2023) · SpectralUnmix and spectropath (2026)

Resolved structure in maps, images, and data cubes

Ridges can bend across a sphere, faint components can remain contiguous across bands, and neighbouring spaxels can change coherently in spectra. The DES analysis adapted subspace-constrained mean shift to weak-lensing mass maps; SCONCE extended ridge finding to spherical and conic geometries.

CAPIVARA maps spectrally similar spaxels back to coherent galaxy regions. SAGUI combines multiscale spatial detection with spectral similarity in multiband images. RadialPaths constructs centre-conditioned profiles for irregular sources with one or more centres.

Selected evidence: DES weak-lensing ridges · SCONCE · CAPIVARA · SAGUI · RadialPaths

Young stellar objects and Galactic structure

The SPICY catalogue identified roughly 120,000 candidate young stellar objects from mid-infrared photometry. Their spatial distribution supported the identification of 25 star-forming regions in a narrow structure about 1 kpc long, with a pitch angle of about 56 degrees.

Selected evidence: SPICY catalogue · Kuhn et al. (2021), A&A 651, L10
