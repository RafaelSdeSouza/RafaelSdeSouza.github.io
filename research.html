Research

Current workResearch trajectorySelected Contributions

My research has moved across scales and subfields rather than followed a single fixed programme. I began with primordial magnetic fields, low-mass haloes and the first stars; later work moved through cosmology, transients, Galactic structure, galaxy populations, nuclear astrophysics and resolved spectroscopy. The recurring problems are statistical rather than tied to one source class: inaccessible likelihoods, selective calibration samples, non-Gaussian observables, and data whose information depends on spectral ordering or spatial structure. My current work focuses on incomplete calibration, representation completion and path-based descriptions of spectra and resolved sources.

Inference beyond observed support

In development

When the target population occupies regions absent from the calibration sample, reweighting can alter the mixture only within observed support. I am studying which population quantities remain bounded under explicit restrictions on the missing conditional law and how those bounds change when limited information about the unobserved population is introduced.

What the representation leaves behind

In development

A representation learned for reconstruction or prediction may discard variation needed by a later physical parameter. The current experiments separate losses introduced by the representation from errors introduced by the downstream estimator. The question is whether the retained coordinates support several plausible scientific uses rather than only the task on which they were trained.

Spectra as ordered geometric objects

Preprint and released package · 2026

spectropath represents a spectral line as an ordered path in velocity–flux space. Profiles with similar widths or moments can remain distinguishable when absorption, asymmetry or multiple components occur in a different sequence along the spectral axis. Toy profiles test these distinctions against line widths and conventional shape coefficients.

Radial structure beyond a single centre

Preprint and released package · 2026

A conventional radial profile assumes that distance from one centre is sufficient. That assumption becomes ambiguous for irregular connected sources or structures with several plausible centres. RadialPaths measures distance through the observed footprint while retaining the centre assigned to each position.

Research trajectory

From pixels to coherent physical regions

Integral-field spectroscopy made spatial dependence impossible to treat as a secondary detail. Independent spaxel fits ignore coherence, while bins constructed only to reach a target signal-to-noise ratio need not follow changes in the continuum or emission lines.

The first work in this direction reconstructed IFU fields with explicit spatial models. CAPIVARA later grouped spaxels by spectral similarity and returned the groups to their positions in the galaxy. In five MaNGA systems, it recovered regions coherent in continuum and emission-line properties without imposing a bulge–disc decomposition. SAGUI extended the same spectro-spatial principle to multiband images.

Galaxy populations across mass and environment

A Bayesian negative-binomial model separated the expected number of globular clusters from excess count variation. Studies of nuclear star clusters found stellar mass to be the principal predictor of nucleation, with environment contributing a secondary effect. J-PLUS imaging expanded the globular-cluster candidate sample around the M81 group.

Other projects measured how ultraviolet-bright red-sequence galaxies change with redshift and stellar mass, and how star formation and stellar populations vary in jellyfish galaxies with mass and stripping intensity. The recurring difficulty was to distinguish a physical population trend from the selection, measurement process or count variation through which it was observed.

Reconstructing the Milky Way from incomplete tracers

The Milky Way projects began with a census problem. Gaia astrometry revealed new nearby open clusters and showed that even the population within roughly one kiloparsec remained incomplete. SPICY extended the census to young stars obscured at optical wavelengths, identifying roughly 120,000 candidates from mid-infrared photometry.

Their spatial distribution supported the identification of 25 star-forming regions in a narrow structure about one kiloparsec long. Its pitch angle of approximately 56 degrees is substantially larger than the value normally assigned to the Sagittarius arm. Later studies moved from young populations to individual stellar histories, inferring birth radii in the Solar neighbourhood and finding that temperature, metallicity and age, rather than radial migration, primarily explain the observed lithium depletion.

From spectral diversity to transient discovery

Supernova spectra made the limitations of standard coordinates particularly visible. Observations are heterogeneous in phase and wavelength coverage, while subtype labels reduce continuous spectral diversity to a few categories. DRACULA used transfer learning, nonlinear dimensional reduction and clustering to compare Type Ia spectra observed under different conditions. The resulting groups occupied parts of a largely continuous distribution rather than isolated clusters.

For Type II supernovae, a graph connecting 1,595 spectra from 145 objects exposed outliers and temporal evolution without imposing fixed class boundaries. Spectra changed rapidly near maximum light and became more homogeneous toward the plateau end. Work with Fink and ELEPHANT then moved the problem into live alert streams, where a scientifically valuable event must be recognised before it fades.

Learning from selective samples

A held-out spectroscopic sample can resemble the training set while both differ from the photometric population to which an estimator will be applied. The Teddy and Happy catalogues separated two causes of failure: missing colour–magnitude support and changes in measurement errors or selection within covered regions.

Active learning shifted the problem from diagnosing a fixed sample to deciding how it should grow. We treated spectroscopic follow-up as a sequential decision: after each observation, the candidate pool, class information, observing cost and remaining telescope time change. RESSPECT tested those decisions under simulated survey conditions. The limitation that now interests me is more severe: further observations within the covered region cannot identify what happens in parts of the target population with no spectroscopic support.

Reaction rates across discordant experiments

The nuclear-astrophysics work posed a different problem: combining measurements of the same reaction when each experiment carries its own normalisation and systematics. A pooled fit can appear precise by suppressing disagreement among experiments instead of propagating it.

The hierarchical analyses estimated the reaction model and experiment-level effects jointly. They covered ³He(d,p)⁴He, tritium–deuterium fusion, ⁷Be(n,p)⁷Li, D(p,γ)³He and ¹⁶O(p,γ)¹⁷F. The ⁷Be analysis obtained rate uncertainties of approximately 1.5–2.0 per cent below 1 GK; the D(p,γ) analysis obtained a 2.2 per cent uncertainty at 0.8 GK, directly affecting the predicted primordial deuterium abundance.

Probability laws for astronomical observables

The generalized-linear-model papers began from a mismatch between familiar regression assumptions and the observables. Whether a minihalo forms one or several Population III stars is discrete. A photometric redshift is positive and continuous. Globular-cluster counts show more variation than a Poisson model permits.

The three studies used binomial, gamma and Bayesian negative-binomial models. The central decision was to choose the probability law from the process generating the observable rather than apply one regression model to all three cases. Bayesian Models for Astrophysical Data extended this argument through complete generalized, hierarchical and likelihood-free analyses, keeping the observed quantity, latent variables and sources of uncertainty explicit.

From cosmological inference to cosmic geometry

Principal-component analyses examined how the number of retained components changes the bias of the reconstructed Hubble parameter. COSMOABC addressed a different obstacle: cluster-count simulations were available, but their likelihood was impractical to evaluate. Approximate Bayesian computation replaced likelihood evaluation with comparisons between simulated and observed summaries.

Density ridges were extracted from DES weak-lensing mass maps as curvilinear structures rather than isolated peaks. SCONCE extended ridge finding to spherical and conic domains, where planar distances distort neighbourhoods and paths.

Making the first stars observable

The work on low-mass haloes led to the environments in which the first stars formed. Simulations tested how halo mass, spin and shape relate to the number and masses of Population III stars. Explosive transients then supplied a possible observational route.

Rate calculations and synthetic surveys examined whether Population III gamma-ray bursts and pair-instability supernovae could be detected with JWST, radio and X-ray missions, or the SKA. The assumed initial-mass function determined both which explosions occurred and how many entered a survey. These studies made detectability part of the physical calculation: the predicted population had to be passed through transient luminosities, event rates, survey depth and cadence before it became an observable one.

Magnetic fields and the smallest dark haloes

I began in theoretical cosmology, asking whether primordial magnetic fields could affect structure on scales far below those normally used to constrain cosmological models. An early study examined whether fluctuations near the quark–hadron transition could supply seed fields for later amplification.

The next question was more directly astrophysical. Magnetic pressure changes how baryons accumulate in shallow dark-matter potentials. The calculations showed that a random primordial field raises the filtering mass and can reduce the gas fraction of low-mass haloes. The important consequence was that some small haloes could remain dark because they failed to acquire enough baryonic material.
